\begin{abstract}
Large metal--organic framework (MOF) screening databases vary simultaneously in
chemistry, network architecture, and pore geometry, making geometry-specific
adsorption effects difficult to isolate. Here, we construct outcome-blind
comparisons in which recorded chemistry and broad framework architecture are fixed
before geometry contrasts or adsorption outcomes are examined. Starting from
279,019 ARC--MOF records, 242,363 charged CIFs were parsed successfully. Recorded-chemistry matching followed by topology and dimensionality refinement
yielded 19,068 validated multi-member architecture groups containing 44,511
frameworks. A geometry-only threshold census was then used to freeze a 10\%
pore-geometry contrast criterion before outcome access, retaining 11,072 families.
One deterministic maximum-separation pair was selected per family, producing 11,072
frozen pairs comprising 22,144 unique frameworks.

Before reading adsorption values, CH$_4$ uptake at 298 K and 65 bar in
\si{\milli\mole\per\gram} was frozen as the confirmatory endpoint. Moving from the lower to the higher value of the prespecified pair-specific
largest-contrast geometry descriptor produced a median uptake change of
\SI{0.584}{\milli\mole\per\gram} (95\% family-bootstrap CI:
\SIrange{0.555}{0.615}{\milli\mole\per\gram}), with 70.5\% of pairs positive.
The effect strengthened with geometry contrast: median changes were
\SI{1.70}{\milli\mole\per\gram} for the $\geq20\%$ tier and
\SI{3.16}{\milli\mole\per\gram} for the $\geq30\%$ tier. Secondary analyses showed
strong pressure dependence: CH$_4$ responses were near zero or negative at lower
pressures but positive at higher loading, whereas several low-pressure CO$_2$
responses were negative and anticorrelated with the primary endpoint. Across nine
low/high-pressure endpoint pairs, 28.0\% of framework pairs were
pressure-activated compared with 6.2\% pressure-suppressed. Geometry effects were
also strongly descriptor-dependent, with pairs labelled ASA or AVA by their largest
geometry contrast favoring positive responses and diameter-labelled pairs more often remaining negative.
The primary response remained positive after simultaneously removing the two
dominant topology classes (pcu and fsc; median
\SI{0.295}{\milli\mole\per\gram}), but collapsed to near zero after removing
ASA- and AVA-driven pairs (median \SI{0.019}{\milli\mole\per\gram}), showing
that the favorable high-pressure response is topology-robust but not
geometry-class universal. Leave-source-out tests retained a positive median
response after removal of every major ARC--MOF source database, and the ASA/AVA
signal likewise survived removal of the dominant sources. However, 99.2\% of the
frozen pairs were hypothetical--hypothetical, so transferability to experimentally
characterized MOFs remains insufficiently sampled. These results show that geometry-associated adsorption
changes are substantial but regime- and descriptor-dependent rather than
universally monotonic.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} metal--organic frameworks; adsorption; pore geometry;
chemistry matching; topology; pressure dependence; ARC--MOF

\section{Introduction}

Metal--organic frameworks (MOFs) offer unusually broad control over chemical
composition, network structure, and pore geometry. The same flexibility that makes
MOFs attractive for adsorption and separation also creates a basic interpretation
problem: across a large screening database, pore diameter, accessible volume,
surface area, density, topology, metal composition, linker chemistry, and operating
conditions usually vary together. A global structure--property model can therefore
predict adsorption accurately without establishing what changes when geometry
varies within an otherwise chemically comparable framework family.

Deliberate control of pore size, accessible volume, and internal surface has long
been central to reticular design. Isoreticular MOF series established that pore
dimensions can be tuned systematically while preserving an underlying framework
motif, with direct consequences for methane storage \cite{eddaoudi2002isoreticular}.
More generally, pore chemistry and geometric confinement jointly determine where
and how adsorbates are accommodated \cite{ji2020porechemistry}. This distinction is
especially important at high loading, where pore filling, accessible volume, and
the thermodynamics of adsorption in confined space become increasingly important
\cite{myers2002highpressure}. Surface-area and pore-size descriptors are therefore
useful structural measures, but they should not be interpreted as interchangeable
or universally monotonic design variables \cite{thommes2015iupac}.

Large computational MOF collections make these relationships accessible at scale,
but they also combine chemically and structurally different source populations.
The known diversity of the MOF structural ecosystem means that database provenance
can correlate with geometry, chemistry, and synthetic status
\cite{moosavi2020ecosystem}. A database-wide geometry trend can therefore reflect
source composition as well as a transferable structure--property relationship.

We address a complementary structure--property question:
how does adsorption vary when recorded chemistry and broad framework architecture
are held fixed while measured pore geometry changes? The analysis is deliberately
organized as an outcome-blind cohort-construction problem. Chemistry is grouped
first, architecture is then constrained using recorded topology and dimensionality,
and only after these definitions are frozen are meaningful geometry contrasts and
framework pairs constructed. Adsorption and process outcomes are excluded from
every cohort-definition step.

ARC--MOF provides a suitable test bed because it combines charged CIF structures,
geometric descriptors, topology and chemistry-cluster assignments, and adsorption
records at large scale \cite{burner2023arcmof,campana2009repeat}. The original
ARC--MOF analysis already emphasized that geometric descriptors become especially
important for uptake as adsorption approaches higher loading, whereas interaction
and electrostatic terms become more influential at lower chemical potential
\cite{burner2023arcmof}. Our question is therefore deliberately narrower than
showing that geometry matters in a database-wide model: we test whether a
high-loading geometry response persists when recorded chemistry and broad
architecture are fixed before outcomes are inspected. The geometric
descriptors are rooted in established high-throughput porous-material analysis
methods \cite{willems2012zeopp}, while automated topology identification provides
a reproducible coarse architectural label \cite{zoubritzky2022crystalnets}. Scale,
however, does not itself provide controlled comparisons. Reused compositions,
incomplete metadata, cluster-label limitations, topology differences, weak
geometry changes, and outcome-informed pair construction can all create misleading
support.

We therefore use explicit, auditable family definitions and freeze the geometry
comparison before opening adsorption values. We first construct same-recorded-
chemistry families and refine them by recorded topology and dimensionality. We then
measure within-family geometry variation, freeze a 10\% pore-geometry threshold,
and select one deterministic maximum-separation pair per family. Only after this
catalogue is frozen do we designate a confirmatory adsorption endpoint and examine
secondary gases, pressures, and process metrics.

The resulting comparisons reveal a reproducible but non-universal geometry-associated response.
High-pressure methane uptake increases for most frozen pairs and the magnitude of
the response rises strongly with geometry contrast. In contrast, lower-pressure
responses are weaker and several CO$_2$ conditions show the opposite sign. The
same framework pair can therefore move from unfavorable or neutral geometry
response at low pressure to favorable response at high pressure. This
pressure-activation behavior is common, depends strongly on the geometric
descriptor that defines the contrast, and varies across topology classes.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Outcome-blind construction of the frozen geometry-pair cohort}

The cohort was constructed without reading adsorption or process outcomes. The
ARC--MOF geometry registry contained 279,019 framework records, of which 242,363
had corresponding charged CIF records. All targeted charged CIFs parsed
successfully, with complete agreement between raw-CIF and parsed element sets.
The parsed structures contained 239,970 single-metal and 2,393 mixed-metal records;
oxidation-state information was not present and was therefore not inferred.

Candidate same-recorded-chemistry families were defined from scale-invariant
whole-CIF elemental composition, scale-invariant metal-element composition, and
ARC--MOF metal-, linker-, and functional-chemistry cluster assignments. Geometry,
topology, dimensionality, geometric-cluster labels, partial charges, and all
adsorption/process outcomes were excluded from the chemistry signature. This
produced 126,062 complete chemistry groups containing 154,150 frameworks, including
20,278 multi-member chemistry families comprising 48,366 frameworks.

Architecture was refined only after chemistry families were frozen. Exact agreement
in recorded topology and dimensionality yielded 19,068 validated same-recorded-
architecture groups containing 44,511 frameworks. Only 27 source chemistry
families split into more than one validated architecture. A further 1,406
frameworks became architecture singletons and 2,449 were ambiguous because
topology and/or dimensionality was missing. Of the validated architecture groups,
18,390 groups containing 41,796 frameworks retained measurable variation in at
least one geometry descriptor.

Because any nonzero variation was too permissive for pair construction, we next
performed an outcome-blind threshold census using eight pore descriptors: ASA,
AVA, AVAf, POAVA, POAVAf, $D_i$, $D_f$, and $D_{\mathrm{if}}$. Candidate 5, 10,
20, and 30\% thresholds retained 14,739, 11,072, 6,948, and 5,115 families,
respectively. The empirical median of the strongest within-family pore span was
12.8\%. Before pair construction or outcome access, 10\% was frozen as the working
criterion; the 20 and 30\% cutoffs were retained as stronger prespecified
sensitivity tiers.

One primary geometry pair was then selected within each 10\%-eligible family by
maximizing the largest symmetric relative difference across the eight pore
descriptors, breaking ties by the mean eight-descriptor difference and then by
framework identifier. This produced exactly 11,072 frozen primary pairs comprising
22,144 unique frameworks. Their median maximum pore difference was 27.2\%, and the
median mean difference across all eight descriptors was 12.8\%. AVA was the
largest-contrast driver for 3,933 pairs, followed by $D_f$ (2,211), ASA (2,036),
and AVAf (1,580).

\subsection{High-pressure methane adsorption responds strongly to geometry}

After the pair catalogue was frozen and audited, outcome-table schemas and
condition coverage were inspected without reading outcome values. All 11,072
frozen pairs were represented at the candidate adsorption conditions. CH$_4$
uptake at 298 K and 65 bar, expressed in \si{\milli\mole\per\gram}, was then frozen
as the confirmatory endpoint before outcome values were opened. Each pair was
oriented from the framework with the lower value of its already-frozen driver
descriptor to the framework with the higher value, and the primary pair effect was
defined as
\[
\Delta q = q_{\mathrm{geometry\mbox{-}high}}-
           q_{\mathrm{geometry\mbox{-}low}}.
\]

All 11,072 pairs had complete primary outcome values. The median geometry-associated
change was \SI{0.5836}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; a 10,000-replicate nonparametric
family bootstrap gave a 95\% interval of
\SIrange{0.5546}{0.6149}{\milli\mole\per\gram}. The mean change was
\SI{2.427}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, and 70.5\% of pairs had $\Delta q>0$.
The median signed symmetric relative effect was 4.90\%.

The response strengthened markedly when the prespecified geometry contrast was
made more stringent. The $\geq20\%$ tier contained 6,948 pairs and had a median
$\Delta q$ of \SI{1.698}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, with 78.3\% positive. The
$\geq30\%$ tier contained 5,115 pairs and had a median $\Delta q$ of
\SI{3.164}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, with 85.2\% positive. The binned relationship
between maximum geometry difference and median adsorption response likewise rose
sharply at the largest geometry contrasts.

The effect depended strongly on which pore descriptor defined the selected
contrast. ASA-driven pairs had a median $\Delta q$ of
\SI{2.996}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and were 92.9\% positive; AVA-driven pairs had a
median of \SI{1.220}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and were 84.2\% positive. In contrast,
pairs driven by $D_f$, $D_i$, and $D_{\mathrm{if}}$ had slightly negative median
changes ($-0.146$, $-0.135$, and $-0.128$ \si{\milli\mole\per\gram},
respectively). Thus the primary result is not equivalent to a universal
``larger-pore-is-better'' rule; the direction and magnitude depend on the type and
size of geometry change.

\subsection{Geometry effects depend on pressure, adsorbate, and process objective}

After completion of the confirmatory analysis, the remaining adsorption and process
outcomes were opened as prespecified secondary analyses while keeping pair
membership and orientation fixed. The clearest recurring pattern was pressure
dependence. In methane storage, the median CH$_4$ response was essentially zero at
5.8 bar ($-0.0047$ \si{\milli\mole\per\gram}; 49.5\% positive) but positive at
65 bar (\SI{0.5836}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; 70.5\% positive). The same pattern
appeared in other CH$_4$ contexts: landfill-gas CH$_4$ shifted from
$-0.0004$ at 0.01 bar to \SI{0.0628}{\milli\mole\per\gram} at 4.4 bar, while
natural-gas-purification CH$_4$ shifted from
$-0.0288$ at 0.9 bar to \SI{0.1933}{\milli\mole\per\gram} at 9 bar.

CO$_2$ behaved differently at low and moderate pressures. Median responses were
negative for landfill CO$_2$ at 0.26 and 3.2 bar, natural-gas-purification CO$_2$
at 0.1 and 1 bar, and post-combustion CO$_2$ at 0.015 and 0.15 bar. The strongest
pressure reversal occurred for pre-combustion CO$_2$: the median response was
$-0.1561$ \si{\milli\mole\per\gram} at 0.4 bar, with 33.2\% positive pairs, but
increased to \SI{0.5486}{\milli\mole\per\gram} at 16 bar, with 66.9\% positive.
N$_2$ and H$_2$ showed smaller positive median responses and are summarized in the
Supporting Information.

The pressure-dependent adsorption pattern propagated unevenly to process metrics.
Median methane-storage working capacity increased by
\SI{0.467}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, with 72.3\% of pairs positive, and
pre-combustion working capacity increased by
\SI{0.660}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, with 69.2\% positive. Landfill,
natural-gas-purification, and post-combustion working-capacity medians were close to
zero or negative. Selectivity, however, decreased in all four separation contexts
with available values: median changes were $-0.363$ for landfill gas, $-0.638$ for
natural-gas purification, $-4.456$ for post-combustion, and $-4.705$ for
pre-combustion. Geometry changes that improve high-loading capacity can therefore
degrade separation selectivity, emphasizing that the relevant geometry direction
depends on the process objective.

\subsection{Pressure activation is common and depends on geometry driver and topology}

To test whether the high-pressure response represented the same pair ordering across
conditions, we compared the frozen 65-bar CH$_4$ effect with all 17 remaining
\si{\milli\mole\per\gram} adsorption endpoints. Concordance with the primary
response varied strongly by condition. Spearman correlations were 0.816 for
pre-combustion H$_2$ at 0.6 bar, 0.777 for pre-combustion CO$_2$ at 16 bar, 0.746
for post-combustion N$_2$ at 0.75 bar, 0.640 for natural-gas-purification CH$_4$
at 9 bar, and 0.585 for landfill CH$_4$ at 4.4 bar.
In contrast, low-pressure CO$_2$ endpoints were negatively correlated with the
primary response, with Spearman coefficients of approximately $-0.28$ to $-0.32$.

Direct low-to-high pressure comparisons showed that sign reversals were common and
asymmetric. For methane storage, 26.8\% of pairs changed from negative at 5.8 bar
to positive at 65 bar, whereas only 5.8\% changed from positive to negative.
Negative-to-positive transitions were similarly common for landfill CH$_4$
(26.6\%) and natural-gas-purification CH$_4$ (26.8\%). Pre-combustion CO$_2$
showed the strongest pressure activation: 43.5\% of pairs changed from negative at
0.4 bar to positive at 16 bar, compared with 9.8\% changing in the opposite
direction.

We therefore summarized each frozen pair across nine lower-pressure and nine
higher-pressure adsorption endpoints. A pair was classified as majority positive
within a regime when more than half of its observed endpoint responses were
positive. Four response archetypes emerged: 3,618 pairs (32.7\%) were positive in
both regimes; 3,100 (28.0\%) were pressure-activated, becoming majority positive
only at higher pressure; 687 (6.2\%) were pressure-suppressed; and 3,667 (33.1\%)
were negative in both regimes. Across individual pairs, the median fraction of
positive endpoints increased from 44.4\% at low pressure to 55.6\% at high
pressure.

Archetype composition depended strongly on the geometry driver. Among ASA-driven
pairs, 57.4\% were positive in both regimes and 34.9\% were pressure-activated,
leaving only 5.2\% negative in both. AVA-driven pairs were especially enriched in
the pressure-activated class (40.4\%). In contrast, more than half of the pairs
driven by $D_f$, $D_i$, or $D_{\mathrm{if}}$ were negative in both regimes.
Topology also modulated pressure activation: among well-supported topology classes,
fsc showed a larger pressure-activated fraction than pcu, nbo, or sra. The
geometry--adsorption relationship is therefore transferable only conditionally;
both the operating regime and the type of geometric perturbation determine whether
a favorable response emerges.

\subsection{The primary effect is topology-robust but concentrated in accessible-area and volume changes}

We finally tested whether the confirmatory high-pressure CH$_4$ result was carried
by the dominant topology classes or by particular geometry-driver populations.
These analyses were secondary robustness tests and preserved the frozen pair
catalogue, orientation, primary endpoint, and estimand (Supporting Information,
Tables~S17--S18).

The pooled result was not dependent on the two dominant topology classes. Removing
pcu alone retained 6,410 pairs and reduced the median $\Delta q$ from
\SI{0.584}{\milli\mole\per\gram} to
\SI{0.478}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, while removal of fsc had essentially no effect
(\SI{0.584}{\milli\mole\per\gram}). More stringently, simultaneous removal of pcu
and fsc retained only 2,191 pairs yet still produced a positive median response of
\SI{0.295}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, with 64.6\% of pairs positive. A descriptive
10,000-replicate family bootstrap gave a 95\% interval of
\SIrange{0.249}{0.351}{\milli\mole\per\gram}. Thus, although pcu and fsc contain
most frozen pairs, they do not by themselves create the positive primary result.

Driver removal gave a different answer. Excluding AVA-driven pairs reduced the
median response to \SI{0.312}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, and excluding ASA-driven pairs
reduced it to \SI{0.364}{\milli\mole\per\gram}. Simultaneously excluding both ASA
and AVA retained 5,103 pairs but reduced the median to only
\SI{0.0185}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; 51.0\% of pairs were positive and the
descriptive bootstrap interval crossed zero
(\SIrange{-0.0080}{0.0415}{\milli\mole\per\gram}). Conversely, removing
$D_f$-driven pairs increased the pooled median to
\SI{0.981}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, consistent with the slightly negative
$D_f$-specific response observed in the primary driver analysis.

The near-zero 10\% result outside ASA/AVA should not be interpreted as evidence
that all other geometry changes are irrelevant. Within the non-ASA/AVA population,
the prespecified stronger-contrast tiers recovered progressively positive
responses: the $\geq20\%$ tier retained 2,384 pairs with median
$\Delta q=\SI{0.168}{\milli\mole\per\gram}$ and 56.0\% positive, while the
$\geq30\%$ tier retained 1,353 pairs with median
$\Delta q=\SI{0.718}{\milli\mole\per\gram}$ and 65.3\% positive. The robustness
analysis therefore sharpens the main conclusion: high-pressure methane uptake is
not generically improved by any increase in pore geometry. The strongest and most
consistent favorable response is associated with accessible surface-area and
accessible-volume changes, while sufficiently large changes in other pore
descriptors can still produce a positive high-pressure response.



\subsection{Database provenance does not create the primary signal, but experimental transfer remains weakly sampled}

Because ARC--MOF combines structures from multiple source databases, we performed a
final provenance stress test using the source prefix encoded in each frozen
framework identifier \cite{burner2023arcmof}. No pair, orientation, threshold,
endpoint, or estimand was changed. Of the 11,072 frozen pairs, 10,844 (97.9\%)
joined frameworks from the same source database. These same-source pairs retained a
median primary response of \SI{0.564}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, close to the full
cohort value. Cross-source pairs were uncommon (228 pairs) and showed much larger
responses; because they represent only 2.1\% of the cohort and may differ
systematically in geometry generation and source construction, their magnitude is
not interpreted as a causal provenance effect.

The cohort was overwhelmingly hypothetical: 10,981 pairs (99.2\%) connected two
hypothetical frameworks, compared with only 82 experimental--experimental pairs and
9 mixed hypothetical--experimental pairs. The hypothetical--hypothetical population
closely reproduced the full result (median
\SI{0.585}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; 70.4\% positive). The small
experimental--experimental subset was directionally consistent (median
\SI{0.355}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; 78.0\% positive), but its size is too small to
support a strong claim of experimental transferability.

No single major source database was necessary for the positive primary result.
DB0 touched 8,261 frozen pairs; after all DB0-containing pairs were removed, the
remaining 2,811 pairs had a median response of
\SI{8.721}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and 84.0\% were positive. Removing DB5, which
touched 2,002 pairs, retained 9,070 pairs with a median response of
\SI{0.308}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and 64.3\% positive. Removing DB6, DB13, or DB1
likewise left positive median responses. These leave-source-out differences show
that effect magnitude varies substantially across source populations, but they
rule out the simpler explanation that the pooled sign is created by one dominant
database.

The ASA/AVA result was similarly not concentrated in one source. Among 5,969
ASA- or AVA-driven pairs, the median primary response was
\SI{1.717}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and 87.2\% were positive. Removing DB0 retained
1,641 ASA/AVA pairs with a median response of
\SI{10.098}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and 96.8\% positive; removing DB5 retained
4,513 pairs with a median response of
\SI{0.984}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and 83.3\% positive. Thus, the favorable
accessible-area/accessible-volume signal is not an artifact of the two largest
source contributions. The large between-source differences are nonetheless an
important applicability boundary rather than evidence that source identity itself
causes the adsorption response.


\subsection{Post-freeze checks clarify descriptor attribution and uptake-basis robustness}

Additional submission-stage analyses were performed without changing the frozen
11,072-pair cohort, pair orientation, geometry threshold, confirmatory endpoint,
or primary estimand. First, we tested how strongly the largest pairwise geometry
difference dominated the second-largest simultaneous descriptor change. Requiring
a top-1/top-2 geometry-difference ratio of at least 1.10 retained 4,603 pairs and
a positive median primary response of \SI{0.414}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; requiring
ratios of at least 1.25 and 1.50 retained 2,866 and 1,715 pairs, with median
responses of \SI{0.187}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and
\SI{0.038}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, respectively. The corresponding positive-pair
fractions fell from 66.3\% to 60.2\% and 53.1\%. Thus, the descriptor stored as the
frozen pair ``driver'' should be read as the descriptor with the largest observed
geometry contrast, not as an isolated causal perturbation. The strongest pooled
responses commonly occur when several pore descriptors change together.

Second, the positive 65-bar CH$_4$ response persisted across uptake bases. The
frozen \si{\milli\mole\per\gram} result was reproduced exactly (median
\SI{0.584}{\milli\mole\per\gram}; 70.5\% positive), while the volumetric response
had a median change of 3.797 v/v units with 60.9\% positive pairs and a family-bootstrap
95\% interval of 3.438--4.185. The wt\% representation was likewise positive
(median 0.934 wt\%; 70.5\% positive). Because wt\% is a monotonic mass-basis
conversion for fixed CH$_4$, the volumetric comparison is the more informative
robustness check: density-related normalization reduces pair-level sign consistency
but does not reverse the population-level result.

Finally, same-source effects were strongly heterogeneous. Among source populations
with at least 100 same-source pairs, DB0 showed a median primary response of
\SI{0.342}{\milli\mole\per\gram} (65.3\% positive), DB5
\SI{10.437}{\milli\mole\per\gram} (99.4\% positive), DB6
\SI{-0.244}{\milli\mole\per\gram} (34.2\% positive), and DB13
\SI{0.466}{\milli\mole\per\gram} (70.2\% positive). These source populations also
differ sharply in geometry regime: the median maximum pair difference was 0.958
for DB5 but 0.180 for DB6, while ASA/AVA-driven pairs comprised 72.2\% and 8.0\%
of those populations, respectively. Source identity is therefore confounded with
geometry magnitude and descriptor composition; these strata define applicability
rather than a causal source effect.

\subsection{Design implications and limits of transferability}

The combined results support a set of bounded geometry-design rules rather than a
single universal pore-size rule. First, pairs whose largest observed geometry contrast was accessible area or accessible
volume were strongly enriched in positive high-pressure methane responses, and
removal of both labels collapsed the pooled 10\% effect to near zero. The practical implication is not simply that ``larger pores'' are favorable,
but that pairs classified by accessible-surface or accessible-volume contrast are
strongly enriched in favorable high-pressure responses. Because several geometry
descriptors often change together, this classification is an attribution label rather
than evidence that one descriptor alone causes the uptake difference.

Second, the usefulness of a geometry change depends strongly on loading regime.
This is physically consistent with the increasing importance of pore filling and
confined-phase thermodynamics at high loading \cite{myers2002highpressure}, but the
present analysis does not decompose those contributions into a unique microscopic
mechanism.
Across the nine paired low/high-pressure adsorption series, the median fraction of
positive responses increased from 44.4\% at low pressure to 55.6\% at high
pressure, and pressure-activated pairs were approximately 4.5 times as common as
pressure-suppressed pairs. The strongest example was pre-combustion CO$_2$, for
which 43.5\% of frozen pairs changed from a negative low-pressure response to a
positive high-pressure response. Geometry should therefore be interpreted in
relation to the thermodynamic regime in which pore filling occurs rather than as a
condition-independent material attribute.

Third, sufficiently large geometry changes can matter even outside the dominant
ASA/AVA classes. Once ASA- and AVA-driven pairs were removed, the 10\% population
showed almost no median response, but the prespecified 20 and 30\% contrast tiers
recovered progressively positive effects. This indicates a distinction between
geometry \emph{type} and geometry \emph{magnitude}: accessible-area and
accessible-volume changes are favorable at moderate contrasts, whereas other pore
changes require larger structural separation before a systematic high-pressure
response emerges.

Fourth, adsorption-capacity gains cannot be assumed to improve process
performance. Methane-storage and pre-combustion working capacities increased on
median, but selectivity decreased in every separation context with available
values. The geometry direction favored by a high-loading uptake objective can
therefore oppose the direction favored by a separation-selectivity objective.
Geometry optimization should consequently be framed against the intended process
metric rather than against uptake alone.

Finally, the positive high-pressure result is not restricted to the dominant
topologies. Simultaneous removal of pcu and fsc retained a clearly positive
primary response, although the median magnitude decreased. Topology therefore
modulates the prevalence of favorable and pressure-activated responses but does
not define a single privileged architecture in which the effect exists. This
supports transfer of the qualitative high-pressure result beyond the two largest
topology classes while preserving the need for topology-specific expectations.

Several observed counterexamples are essential to these rules: low-pressure
CO$_2$ responses can oppose the high-pressure CH$_4$ response, diameter-driven
pairs have slightly negative primary medians, and selectivity can decrease while
working capacity increases. These results directly reject a universal
``larger-pore-is-better'' interpretation.

These rules deliberately stop short of causal or atomistic interpretation. The
matched families control recorded composition, chemistry-cluster assignments,
topology, and dimensionality, but they do not constitute controlled mechanical
deformations of one experimentally realized crystal. Likewise, the frozen driver
descriptor identifies the largest measured geometric separation within a pair; it
does not prove that this descriptor alone causes the adsorption difference.
Accordingly, the central claim of this work is conditional: within tightly matched
recorded MOF families, geometry-associated adsorption differences are reproducible
and often substantial, but their direction depends on the form and magnitude of
the geometry change, the adsorbate and pressure regime, and the process objective.
The source-provenance analysis further shows that this conclusion is not created by
one dominant ARC--MOF database, while also making clear that the present evidence
is primarily evidence within hypothetical structure space rather than an
experimental validation set.


\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The analysis inherits the scope and assumptions of the underlying ARC--MOF
simulation resource \cite{burner2023arcmof}. The reported adsorption responses are
equilibrium simulation outcomes for recorded framework structures; they do not
measure synthesis yield, activation success, defect populations, phase purity,
mechanical stability, framework breathing, or kinetic transport. The underlying
molecular simulations use classical interaction models and assigned partial
charges, so force-field and charge-model uncertainty are not propagated through
the matched-pair estimates. The geometry descriptors likewise summarize pore
accessibility and size but do not uniquely specify local adsorption-site energetics.

The pressure analysis is based on the discrete pressure conditions available in the
ARC--MOF tables. Terms such as ``pressure activated'' therefore describe a
repeatable change in sign prevalence between the designated lower- and
higher-pressure endpoints; they do not identify a continuous thermodynamic
transition pressure. Similarly, process metrics are derived equilibrium-screening
quantities and should not be interpreted as complete process simulations.

Database composition is an additional limitation. Although leave-source-out tests
show that no major ARC--MOF source is required for the positive high-pressure
response, 99.2\% of the frozen pairs connect two hypothetical frameworks. The
experimental--experimental subset contains only 82 pairs. Consequently, the
database-wide and source-robust conclusions are strongest as statements about the
sampled ARC--MOF structure space, while experimental generalization requires
targeted validation in synthesized, activated, and structurally characterized
families.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data sources, registry construction, and identifier harmonisation}

The analysis uses the ARC--MOF framework inventory, geometric descriptors,
dimensionality assignments, topology and chemistry-cluster tables, charged CIF
archive, RAC descriptors, adsorption records, and process tables
\cite{burner2023arcmof,willems2012zeopp,janet2017rac}. A canonical framework
registry was constructed from the ARC--MOF subset of the geometric-property table.
Source identifiers were normalized by explicit deterministic rules rather than row
position. Each auxiliary source was joined through the canonical framework
identifier, with duplicate keys, normalization collisions, unmatched identifiers,
coverage, and schema requirements audited explicitly.

\subsection{Charged-CIF chemistry extraction}

The charged CIF archive was streamed without extraction. For each registry
framework with CIF coverage, CIF bytes were associated with the canonical
framework identifier and hashed for provenance. Structures were parsed with
pymatgen \cite{ong2013pymatgen} without primitive-cell reduction. The extraction
recorded formula and elemental composition, normalized element fractions,
metal-element composition, site count, cell volume and density,
disorder/partial-occupancy flags, oxidation-state evidence, and raw charge-tag
metadata. Metal classification refers only to element class and does not assert
framework-node membership. No bonds, framework-connected linkers, or nodes were
inferred.

\subsection{Candidate same-recorded-chemistry families}

The chemistry signature comprised scale-invariant whole-CIF elemental composition,
scale-invariant metal-element composition, and ARC--MOF metal-, linker-, and
functional-chemistry cluster assignments. Eligibility required a successful and
internally consistent CIF parse, one ordered structure, at least one metal element,
complete RAC evidence, and valid nonnegative chemistry-cluster labels. Negative
cluster assignments were treated as unique and were never grouped. Mixed-metal
structures were retained only when normalized whole-CIF and metal-element
compositions and all three chemistry-cluster assignments matched. Topology,
dimensionality, geometric-cluster identity, measured geometry, partial charges, and
adsorption/process outcomes were excluded from the chemistry signature.

\subsection{Same-recorded-architecture refinement}

Architecture was refined after chemistry families were frozen. The architecture key
consisted of chemistry-family identifier, recorded topology, and recorded
dimensionality. Missing topology or dimensionality was treated as ambiguous rather
than imputed. No CIFs were reparsed, no bonds were inferred, and no
local-environment algorithm was used. Measured geometry did not enter the
architecture key. Recorded topology was treated as a practical coarse network label
consistent with automated topological-identification practice
\cite{zoubritzky2022crystalnets}.

\subsection{Geometry variation, threshold selection, and pair construction}

For each validated architecture group, geometry was described using unit-cell
volume, density, ASA, AVA, AVAf, POAVA, POAVAf, $D_i$, $D_f$, and
$D_{\mathrm{if}}$. Scientific thresholding used only the eight pore descriptors,
excluding unit-cell volume and density. For descriptor $x$, the family fractional
span was
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{rel}}(x)=
\frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}
{\max\left(|x_{\min}|,|x_{\max}|\right)}.
\]
Candidate thresholds of 5, 10, 20, and 30\% were evaluated without reading
adsorption/process outcomes. The 10\% threshold was frozen before pair construction;
20 and 30\% were retained as stronger-contrast sensitivity tiers.

Within each 10\%-eligible family, all unordered framework pairs were enumerated.
For pore descriptor $k$, the pairwise relative difference was
\[
d_k(i,j)=
\frac{|x_{ik}-x_{jk}|}
{\max\left(|x_{ik}|,|x_{jk}|\right)},
\]
with zero assigned when both values were zero. The selected pair maximized
$D_{\max}(i,j)=\max_k d_k(i,j)$. Ties were resolved by the larger mean
eight-descriptor difference and then lexicographically by framework identifier.
Exactly one pair was retained per family. The descriptor attaining $D_{\max}$ was
stored as the frozen pair driver and later used to orient the pair from lower to
higher driver-descriptor value.

\subsection{Outcome inventory and confirmatory endpoint freezing}

Before outcome values were read, the adsorption/process schemas and framework
coverage were inventoried using only identifier and condition metadata. The nine
adsorption tables contained two pressure conditions each and covered all 22,144
frozen pair members at nearly all conditions. The process table covered 22,080
frozen members, corresponding to 11,008 complete pairs per process.

The confirmatory endpoint was frozen as CH$_4$ uptake at 298 K and 65 bar in
\si{\milli\mole\per\gram}. The pair orientation was the already-frozen
driver-low to driver-high direction. No imputation was permitted; a pair
contributed only if both members had observed values. The primary pair effect was
\[
\Delta q=q_{\mathrm{high}}-q_{\mathrm{low}}.
\]
The primary population estimator was the median $\Delta q$ across frozen families.
No minimum ``practically meaningful'' uptake difference was prespecified; the
confirmatory target was the magnitude and sign of the family-level median under
the frozen endpoint and pair definition. Uncertainty was estimated with a
nonparametric percentile bootstrap that resampled whole frozen families 10,000
times using a fixed random seed of 17. This bootstrap quantifies variation across
matched families; it does not propagate the per-framework Monte Carlo sampling
uncertainty reported by the source simulations. The 20 and 30\% geometry tiers were
prespecified sensitivity populations.

Supporting same-endpoint summaries included the mean signed difference, fraction
of positive pairs, signed symmetric relative effect,
\[
r_{\mathrm{sym}}=
\frac{2(q_{\mathrm{high}}-q_{\mathrm{low}})}
{|q_{\mathrm{high}}|+|q_{\mathrm{low}}|},
\]
with zero assigned when both uptakes were zero, and the log uptake ratio where both
values were strictly positive.

\subsection{Secondary adsorption and process analyses}

After completion and audit of the confirmatory analysis, the remaining adsorption
and process outcomes were treated as secondary/exploratory. Pair identities,
orientation, geometry threshold, and primary endpoint were not changed.
Adsorption endpoints included \si{\milli\mole\per\gram}, v/v, wt\%, heat of
adsorption, and mixture selectivity where available. Process endpoints included
uptake, working capacity, selectivity, purity, SSP, and AFM. For every secondary
endpoint the signed effect was calculated using the same frozen direction,
$\Delta=y_{\mathrm{high}}-y_{\mathrm{low}}$, together with the symmetric relative
effect. These analyses were descriptive/exploratory rather than a family of
confirmatory null-hypothesis tests; no multiplicity-adjusted significance testing
was used, and no secondary endpoint was promoted to confirmatory status after value
inspection. The complete audited secondary summaries are reported in the
Supporting Information rather than only the subsets used in main-text figures.

\subsection{Cross-condition concordance and response archetypes}

For cross-condition analysis, the 18 \si{\milli\mole\per\gram} adsorption endpoints
were arranged as one pair-level effect matrix. Each endpoint was compared with the
primary 65-bar CH$_4$ response using Spearman correlation, sign agreement, positive
fraction, and directional sign-transition frequencies. Within each source/guest
series, the lower-pressure endpoint was compared directly with its higher-pressure
counterpart.

The nine lower-pressure and nine higher-pressure endpoints were then summarized per
frozen pair. A pair was ``majority positive'' within a regime when strictly more
than half of its observed endpoint effects were positive. Four deterministic
archetypes were defined: positive in both regimes; pressure-activated (not majority
positive at low pressure but majority positive at high pressure);
pressure-suppressed (the reverse); and negative in both regimes. The activation
score was the high-pressure positive fraction minus the low-pressure positive
fraction. Driver- and topology-specific archetype frequencies were calculated
without redefining pair membership.


\subsection{Database-provenance robustness}

ARC--MOF source provenance was recovered from the \texttt{DBx-} prefix encoded in
each frozen framework identifier, following the source mapping reported for the
database \cite{burner2023arcmof}. DB12 and DB14 were treated as experimental-source
collections, whereas DB0, DB1, DB2, DB3, DB4, DB5, DB6, DB7, DB8, DB10, DB13,
and DB15 were treated as hypothetical/computationally generated sources. Each
frozen pair was classified as same-source or cross-source and as
hypothetical--hypothetical, experimental--experimental, or mixed provenance.

For each source represented in at least 100 frozen pairs, a leave-one-source-out
stress test removed every pair for which either member came from that source and
recomputed the frozen primary summaries without changing pair definitions or
orientation. The same analysis was repeated within the ASA/AVA-driven population
because the preceding robustness analysis identified those driver classes as the
main contributors to the pooled 10\% response. These analyses were descriptive
robustness tests; source labels were not interpreted as causal variables because
database provenance is correlated with structure-generation protocol, chemical
space, and geometry distributions.


\subsection{Post-freeze submission robustness and structural case selection}

Three additional robustness analyses were performed after the frozen confirmatory
and secondary workflow had been completed. None changed pair membership, pair
orientation, geometry thresholds, endpoint definitions, or estimands. Geometry-attribution
stability ranked the eight frozen pairwise descriptor differences within each pair
and compared the largest difference with the second largest. Prespecified dominance
cuts of 1.10, 1.25, and 1.50 were applied to the top-1/top-2 ratio, and the frozen
primary CH$_4$ summaries were recomputed within each retained subset.

Uptake-basis robustness re-linked the same frozen pairs to the 298 K, 65 bar methane
records and first required exact reproduction of the frozen \si{\milli\mole\per\gram}
pair effects. The same orientation was then used for the v/v and wt\% fields, with
family-level medians, positive fractions, and 10,000-replicate percentile bootstrap
intervals calculated on each basis. Density-stratified summaries were treated as
descriptive checks rather than new confirmatory estimands.

Source-stratified analysis was restricted to same-source frozen pairs and reported
source populations with at least 100 pairs. For each source, the primary response,
maximum geometry-contrast distribution, and ASA/AVA composition were summarized
together so that source heterogeneity was not interpreted without its accompanying
geometry-space differences.

Six structural examples were selected deterministically from the already-frozen
pair catalogue for visualization only: pressure-activated ASA, pressure-activated
AVA, positive-both accessible-space, negative-both diameter, pressure-suppressed,
and experimental-source strata. Selection targeted representative cases within each
predeclared stratum rather than the largest adsorption effect, and no pair was used
for more than one panel. The 12 corresponding CIFs were extracted with SHA256
checksums for downstream rendering; these examples do not alter the population-level
analysis.

\subsection{Audit and reproducibility}

Every completed stage wrote a manifest and deterministic framework-, group-, pair-,
or endpoint-level tables. Independent audit scripts reconstructed target sets,
grouping keys, group sizes, exclusions, source hashes, threshold flags, pair
optimality, outcome linkage, bootstrap results, secondary effect arithmetic,
cross-condition matrices, archetype assignments, dominant-context stress tests,
and database-provenance labels and leave-source-out summaries. The final audits confirmed
that chemistry and architecture definitions were fixed before geometry
thresholding, pair membership was fixed before outcome access, the confirmatory
endpoint was frozen before reading values, and all later secondary analyses
preserved these choices.

\section{Conclusions}

Outcome-blind matching provides a large controlled comparison set for separating
geometry-associated adsorption changes from simultaneous changes in recorded
chemistry and broad architecture. From 279,019 ARC--MOF records, the workflow
ultimately produced 11,072 frozen geometry pairs comprising 22,144 unique
frameworks. At the prespecified high-pressure methane endpoint, moving from the lower to
the higher value of the frozen pair-specific largest-contrast geometry descriptor was associated with a
median uptake increase of
\SI{0.584}{\milli\mole\per\gram}, with a narrow family-bootstrap interval and
70.5\% of pairs positive. Stronger geometry contrasts produced substantially larger
and more consistently positive responses.

The favorable high-pressure response is robust to topology composition but not
universal across geometry classes. Simultaneous removal of pcu and fsc left only
2,191 pairs yet retained a clearly positive median response of
\SI{0.295}{\milli\mole\per\gram}. In contrast, removal of ASA- and AVA-driven pairs
reduced the 10\% primary-tier response to near zero. This identifies ASA/AVA-labelled contrasts as the pair classes most strongly
enriched in the pooled high-pressure methane response, without assigning an isolated
causal role to either descriptor. Other geometry classes are not uniformly
inactive, however: among non-ASA/AVA pairs, the $\geq30\%$ contrast tier still
showed a median increase of \SI{0.718}{\milli\mole\per\gram} and 65.3\% positive
pairs.

Operating condition remains equally important. Low-pressure CH$_4$ responses were
weak, several low-pressure CO$_2$ conditions were unfavorable, and selectivity
often decreased even when high-loading capacity improved. Across the full set of
low/high-pressure adsorption comparisons, pressure activation was approximately
4.5 times as common as pressure suppression. The resulting design picture is
therefore conditional: ASA/AVA-labelled contrasts are most consistently associated
with favorable high-pressure uptake in this cohort, sufficiently large contrasts in
other geometry classes can also be favorable, and capacity gains cannot be assumed
to translate into better separation metrics.

Submission-stage checks further show that the positive high-pressure response survives volumetric
normalization, whereas increasingly isolated single-descriptor contrasts show a much weaker pooled
response. This reinforces a multidimensional pore-change interpretation rather than a causal
single-descriptor rule. Source-stratified effects are likewise heterogeneous because ARC--MOF
source populations occupy different geometry regimes.

These results support a practical interpretation of pore geometry as an
operating-condition-dependent design variable rather than a single monotonic
descriptor. The positive primary sign survives removal of each major ARC--MOF
source, and the ASA/AVA signal is likewise not confined to DB0 or DB5. At the same
time, the cohort is overwhelmingly hypothetical, so this source robustness should
not be confused with experimental validation. The family definitions remain
representation-level controls rather than proof of atom-by-atom structural
identity, and the observed contrasts should therefore be interpreted as
geometry-associated differences within tightly matched recorded families rather
than causal deformation experiments.

\section*{Reproducibility}

The complete analysis has been organized as a coauthor handover repository at
\url{https://github.com/alimardani76/isochemical_mof}. The repository contains the
stage-by-stage analysis scripts, independent audit scripts, frozen result tables,
figure-production source code, final figures, manuscript and Supporting Information
source, a software-environment lock, and file-level provenance manifests. The
workflow is intended to be rerun sequentially: each analytical stage writes
deterministic outputs and is followed by an independent reconstruction audit before
the next stage is treated as complete.

The reported results depend on frozen analytical definitions that should not be
changed silently during reproduction: the recorded-chemistry key, recorded
topology/dimensionality refinement, the 10\% primary geometry threshold, the
deterministic one-pair-per-family rule, pair orientation, the confirmatory
CH$_4$ endpoint at 298 K and 65 bar, the median family-level estimand, and the
10,000-replicate bootstrap with seed 17. The repository-level
\texttt{HANDOVER.md} documents these decisions, the final scientific results,
known limitations, and the required downstream reruns if any frozen step is
modified.

Original ARC--MOF source data are not duplicated in the code repository. The
analysis used ARC--MOF release v7 (Zenodo record 16802743), including the charged
CIF archive and the topology, dimensionality, geometry, RAC/cluster, adsorption,
and process tables described in the Supporting Information. Exact required source
filenames, local source hashes, repository file hashes, and any large result files
kept outside ordinary Git tracking are recorded under
\texttt{data\_manifest/} and \texttt{provenance/}. Figures are regenerated from
the frozen result tables using the plotting scripts in the repository and are not
manually edited analytical outputs.
